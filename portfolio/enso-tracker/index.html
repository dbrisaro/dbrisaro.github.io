How El Niño is monitored

Of all the indices tracked here, Niño-3.4 is the one that matters most. It needs to stay above +0.5 °C for five consecutive overlapping seasons before an El Niño is officially declared. The Southern Oscillation Index (SOI), a measure of the atmospheric pressure difference between Tahiti and Darwin, acts as a cross-check: if the atmosphere has responded to the ocean warming, it will have turned sharply negative too.

The four Niño regions are fixed boxes in the equatorial Pacific, each capturing a different slice of surface temperature. The SOI measures the pressure difference between Darwin and Tahiti. When that gradient reverses, trade winds weaken, warm water spreads east, and what starts as an ocean anomaly gets amplified by the atmosphere until you have a full El Niño.

Moderate El Niño is established. Niño-3.4 sits at +0.94 °C and the eastern Pacific (Niño-1+2 at +1.81 °C) is running 1.8x warmer than the central Pacific, a classic Eastern Pacific signature.

Not all El Niños are the same. In an Eastern Pacific event the warmth sits off South America (Niño-1+2 and Nino-3); in a Central Pacific event it pools near the dateline (Nino-4). Right now Niño-1+2 (+1.81 °C) is running 1.8x warmer than Nino-4 (+0.98 °C), a strong Eastern Pacific signature consistent with 1997-98. EP events tend to be stronger and longer-lived than CP events.

By relative forcing (RONI)

The Relative ONI subtracts the tropical-mean SST warming (20°N–20°S) before comparing events. This removes the global warming background, so two events match when the ENSO forcing itself is similar, regardless of whether one occurred in a cooler era. Events from the 1960s and 1970s appear here because the shape of their forcing anomaly, not their absolute temperature, resembles what 2026 is doing. If you want to know what kind of atmospheric response to expect, RONI is the more honest comparison.

Niño 1+2 captures the far eastern Pacific, just off Ecuador and Peru. In Eastern Pacific events this region warms first and most sharply, often leading the Niño-3.4 signal by several months. The 2026 jump from -0.3 °C in March to +1.81 °C in May is one of the fastest accelerations on record for this index, and it places 2026 well above where 2023-24 was at the same stage. That divergence is significant: it points toward an event with a stronger eastern signature, closer to the 1997-98 footprint.

Dashed blue is estimated from the Niño-3.4 forecast using typical EP scaling ratios.

The SOI provides the atmospheric side of the story. A strong El Niño is not just warm ocean temperatures. The atmosphere has to respond too. When the SOI turns sharply negative, trade winds weaken, which allows warm water to spread east, which further weakens the winds. This Bjerknes feedback is what turns an ocean anomaly into a full event. The 2026 SOI has been tracking close to the 1997-98 pattern through the observed period, suggesting the coupling is already well established.

El Niño threshold is -8, La Niña is +8. Source: NOAA CPC.

What's coming

The surface signal is now clear, but what happens next depends on heat stored below the thermocline. Three additional variables would sharpen the peak intensity forecast considerably.

Kelvin waves (subsurface): three downwelling Kelvin waves in late 2025 and early 2026 loaded the thermocline and are the primary subsurface driver of this event. Depth-section data from GODAS and TAO-TRITON would make this visible.
Warm Water Volume (WWV): the volume of water above 20 °C averaged over 5S-5N, 120E-80W predicts peak intensity months in advance and is available at NOAA PMEL.
OLR (Outgoing Longwave Radiation): negative OLR anomalies over the western Pacific warm pool confirm that convection has coupled to the SST anomaly, closing the Bjerknes feedback loop.
AMJ 2026 ONI: the April-May-June running mean will be the first seasonal value fully within El Niño territory and will sharpen the intensity estimate.
